## Why this film, and why *Praxinoscope*

In 1877, Émile Reynaud invented the *praxinoscope* — a spinning drum of mirrors that sliced continuous motion into discrete flashes of light. Exactly twenty-eight years earlier, in July 1849, Hippolyte Fizeau stood in a cupola in Suresnes and aimed a lantern through a spinning **720-tooth brass wheel** toward a mirror on Montmartre **8,633 meters** away — slicing a beam of light into **55.1-microsecond** flashes to clock the speed of light on Earth for the first time.

That terrestrial wheel was the climax of a 173-year scientific detective story that began at the Paris Observatory in **August 1676**, when 32-year-old Danish astronomer **Ole Rømer** predicted to the French Royal Academy of Sciences that Jupiter's volcanic moon **Io** would emerge from Jupiter's shadow on **November 9, 1676 at 5:35:45 PM** — **ten full minutes late** compared to Jean-Dominique Cassini's tables (`5:25:45 PM`) — because light takes finite time to cross Earth's orbit.

This film unites **12 bespoke painterly 16:9 historical plates** with **25 procedural 30 fps mathematical & optical simulations** (Earth–Jupiter–Io heliocentric light-time geometry, Rømer's 40-eclipse residual curve, James Bradley's 1728 `20.49"` stellar aberration vector diagram on `γ Draconis`, and Fizeau's 720-tooth brass chopper wheel at `12.60 / 25.20 / 37.80 rps`), narrated in **dual-voice English TTS** (`en-US-BrianNeural` + `en-GB-RyanNeural` archival reader) over an **original 48 kHz stereo polyphonic score**.
