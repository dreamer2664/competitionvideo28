| `S01` (`00:00–00:13`) | On Nov 9, 1676 at the Paris Observatory, Cassini's tables predicted Jupiter's innermost moon Io would emerge from Jupiter's shadow at `5:25:45 PM`, but the moon did not appear until `5:35:45 PM` — ten minutes late. | **[ROM76]** & **[SHB98]** / Bobis & Lequeux (2008) p. 99: November 9, 1676 emersion observed at `5h 35m 45s PM` vs. `5h 25m 45s PM` predicted from August 1676 orbits. |
| `S02` (`00:13–00:26`) | Eleven weeks earlier, in August 1676, 32-year-old Danish astronomer Ole Rømer predicted that exact 10-minute delay to the French Royal Academy of Sciences. | **[SHB98]** (Bobis & Lequeux 2008, p. 99): Rømer announced the prediction to the Academy in August 1676 (`Rømer born Sept 25, 1644 -> age 31/32`). |
| `S03` (`00:26–00:39`) | Rømer argued the delay was caused not by an irregularity in Io's orbit, but because light takes finite time to cross Earth's orbit — a claim resisted by Cassini for decades and proved terrestrial-side in 1849 with a 720-tooth brass wheel. | **[ROM76]**, **[SHB98]**, **[FIZ49]**. |
| `S04` (`00:39–00:52`) | From Aristotle and Kepler to René Descartes (1634 letter to Beeckman), prevailing orthodoxy held that light propagates instantaneously (`infinite speed`). | **[SHB98]** & Descartes letter to Isaac Beeckman (Aug 22, 1634): Descartes declared that if any time delay were observed in eclipses, his philosophy would be shaken to its foundations. |
| `S05` (`00:52–01:05`) | Galileo's 1638 *Two New Sciences* proposed uncovering shuttered lanterns across hilltops a mile apart, concluding only that light is if not instantaneous, extraordinarily rapid. | Galileo Galilei (1638), *Discorsi e Dimostrazioni Matematiche intorno a due nuove scienze*, Day 1. |
| `S06` (`01:05–01:18`) | At `299,792 km/s`, a one-mile (`1.61 km`, `3.22 km` round-trip) light trip takes `10.7 microseconds`, whereas human reaction time is ~`0.2 seconds` (`200,000 µs`) — roughly 18,000 times too slow. | Exact kinematics: `3.2187 km / 299,792.458 km/s = 10.736 µs`. |
| `S07` (`01:18–01:31`) | Longitude at sea requires knowing reference-meridian time alongside local solar time (`15° per hour`), and 17th-century pendulum clocks lost accuracy on pitching ship decks. | **[SHB98]** & Bobis & Lequeux (2008) pp. 97–98. |
| `S08` (`01:31–01:44`) | Io orbits Jupiter with a synodic period of `42 hours, 28 minutes, and 35 seconds` (`1.769 days`), producing regular eclipses visible simultaneously from anywhere Jupiter is above the horizon. | **[ROM76]** (`42½ hours`) & **[SHB98]** (`T_syn = 1.76986 days ≈ 42h 28m 36s`). |
| `S09` (`01:44–01:57`) | Between 1671 and 1676, Jean Picard, Cassini, and Rømer timed Io's eclipses from Tycho Brahe's Uraniborg site (`Hven`) and Paris, leading to the 1682/1693 *Carte de France Corrigée* that narrowed France's Atlantic coastline. | **[SHB98]** & Bobis & Lequeux (2008) pp. 97–98. |
| `S10` (`01:57–02:11`) | Rømer and Cassini analyzed roughly 40 immersions and emersions of Io recorded between 1668 and 1676. | **[ROM76]** (*"observations of eight years"*, 1668–1676) & Bobis & Lequeux (2008) manuscript analysis of ~60 total eclipses (~40 through 1676). |
| `S11` (`02:11–02:25`) | Because Jupiter casts its shadow away from the Sun, observers on Earth see *immersions* (Io entering shadow, `C`) while Earth approaches Jupiter (`G -> H`) and *emersions* (Io exiting shadow, `D`) while Earth recedes (`K -> L`). | **[ROM76]** Fig. 1 (`A` Sun, `B` Jupiter, `C` entry into shadow, `D` exit from shadow, `E-F-G-H-K-L` Earth's orbit). |
| `S12` (`02:25–02:40`) | Across 40 consecutive revolutions of Io (`~70.8 days`), Earth moves away from Jupiter along arc `K -> L`, adding millions of kilometers to each successive light path so the apparent period lengthens (`Δt = Δd / c`). | **[ROM76]** p. 234 (*"forty revolutions... perceptibly longer on the side of departure than on the other"*). |
| `S13` (`02:40–02:54`) | On December 7, 1676, Rømer published his diagram in the *Journal des Sçavans*, concluding that light takes about `11 minutes` to cross the radius of Earth's orbit (`~22 minutes` across the diameter; modern true value: `8m 19s` per AU, `16m 38s = 998.8 s` across `2 AU`). | **[ROM76]** p. 234 (`~11 minutes` for Earth's orbital radius / `~22 minutes` across the diameter) & **[SHB98]** (`998.8 s` modern `2 AU` light time). |
| `S14` (`02:54–03:07`) | In his 1690 *Traité de la Lumière*, Christiaan Huygens combined Rømer's 22-minute diameter delay with Cassini's solar parallax (`9.5"`, solar distance `~12,000 Earth diameters`) to calculate the first numerical speed of light (`>600,000×` sound, `~214,000 km/s`). | **[HUY90]** Chapter 1, pp. 8–9. |
| `S15` (`03:07–03:20`) | Cassini rejected Rømer's hypothesis because Jupiter's three outer Galilean moons (`Europa, Ganymede, Callisto`) had more complex orbital inclinations that did not show the same simple residual table, leaving the French Academy skeptical for 52 years. | Bobis & Lequeux (2008) pp. 101–103. |
| `S16` (`03:20–03:33`) | Between 1725 and 1728, English astronomer James Bradley observed the zenith star `γ Draconis` (`Gamma Draconis`) with a vertical telescope mounted in a chimney at Kew and Wanstead, finding it shifted south in March and north in September by `~20 arcseconds` (`20.495"` modern). | **[BRA28]** pp. 638–653. |
| `S17` (`03:33–03:46`) | Bradley realized this was **stellar aberration**: just as falling rain requires tilting an umbrella forward when moving, Earth's orbital speed (`v ≈ 29.8 km/s`) tilts incoming starlight by `tan θ = v / c ≈ 1 / 10,210`, proving light takes `8 minutes 12 seconds` from the Sun (`c ≈ 301,000 km/s`). | **[BRA28]** pp. 646–653 (`10,210 to 1`, `8' 12"` solar light-time). |
| `S18` (`03:46–03:59`) | In July 1849, French physicist Hippolyte Fizeau performed the first non-astronomical measurement of the speed of light between a cupola in Suresnes and a collimator mirror on Montmartre in Paris, `8,633 meters` away (`17,266 m` round-trip). | **[FIZ49]** pp. 90–92 (`8633 mètres`, `Suresnes` to `Montmartre`). |
| `S19` (`03:59–04:13`) | Fizeau's apparatus focused a bright lamp through a semi-reflecting glass plate and between the teeth of a brass wheel cut with exactly `N = 720 teeth` (`720 teeth + 720 gaps = 1,440 divisions`). | **[FIZ49]** p. 91 (`une roue dentée portant 720 dents`). |
| `S20` (`04:13–04:28`) | At rest or low speed, the light pulse returns in `Δt = 2L / c ≈ 55.1 microseconds` through the same gap; as the wheel reaches `f₁ = 12.6 revolutions per second`, the adjacent brass tooth rotates by `1 / 1,440` of a turn during the round-trip and eclipses the returning star-point. | **[FIZ49]** p. 92 (`12,6 tours par seconde` first eclipse; `c = 2 · L · 2N · f₁ = 2 × 8,633 m × 1,440 × 12.6 s⁻¹ = 313,274,304 m/s`, reported by Fizeau in rounds as `70,948 leagues/s` ≈ `315,300 km/s`). |
| `S21` (`04:28–04:42`) | Doubling the speed to `25.2 rps` lets the returning light pass through the *next* gap (`re-brightening`), and tripling to `37.8 rps` produces a second eclipse — yielding `~315,000 km/s` (within ~5% of the true terrestrial value). | **[FIZ49]** p. 92. |
| `S22` (`04:42–04:55`) | Léon Foucault replaced the toothed wheel with a steam-driven rotating mirror in 1862 (`298,000 ± 500 km/s`), and Albert A. Michelson timed an octagonal mirror over a `35 km` baseline between Mount Wilson and Mount San Antonio in 1926 (`299,796 ± 4 km/s`). | **[BIPM83]** historical notes; Michelson (1927), *Astrophysical Journal*, 65, 1–14. |
| `S23` (`04:55–05:09`) | By the 1970s, stabilized helium-neon laser measurements of `c` (`299,792,458 ± 1.2 m/s`) were limited only by the fuzziness of the krypton-86 meter definition itself. | **[BIPM83]** & Evenson et al. (1972), *Phys. Rev. Lett.*, 29, 1346. |
| `S24–S25` (`05:09–05:24`) | On October 20, 1983, the 17th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) fixed `c = 299,792,458 m/s` exact and redefined the meter as the distance light travels in `1 / 299,792,458` of a second. | **[BIPM83]** CGPM 1983 Resolution 1. |
